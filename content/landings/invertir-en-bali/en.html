Why invest in Bali?

Bali is one of the most in-demand holiday destinations in Asia, and well-located land is limited. That combination supports rental occupancy and property values in established areas.

Tourism: the numbers

  • According to the Bali statistics office (BPS Bali), the island received 6.95 million direct foreign arrivals in 2025, up 9.7% on 2024 (6.33 million) and an all-time record.
  • Australia accounts for almost one in four international arrivals, followed by Asian markets (India, China, Malaysia, Singapore, Japan) and Europe.
  • On top of foreign visitors there is strong domestic tourism and a growing community of long-stay residents and digital nomads.

Holiday rentals live off that demand. A one- or two-bedroom villa with a private pool fits the most common guest profile: couples and travellers staying from one week to a month.

What Bali offers an investor

  • A moderate entry ticket. Around €90,000-110,000 buys a new one-bedroom unit in a tourist area.
  • A holiday-rental market built around villas, with its own licensing rules (covered below).
  • Diversification outside your home currency and property market.
  • Personal use. You can block your own weeks and rent out the rest.

No small print

Bali is not a risk-free investment. The right you buy has an end date, rental regulation is getting stricter, the rupiah moves and some developers never finish their projects. We cover all of this in the risks section. If anyone promises you a guaranteed return, walk away.

Can foreigners buy property in Bali?

Foreigners cannot hold freehold land (Hak Milik) in their own name; it is reserved for Indonesian citizens. An investor has three legal routes:

StructureWhat it isTermBest for
Leasehold (Hak Sewa)Long-term lease of the land and the building, signed before a notaryUsually 20-35 years, with an agreed extension optionInvestors buying a villa or apartment to rent out and use. The most common route
Hak PakaiRegistered right of use, available to foreign residents for their own homeUp to 30 years + renewalsPeople living in Bali with a residence permit
PT PMA + HGBForeign-owned Indonesian company holding a right to build (HGB)30 years + renewalsLarger investments or business activity (several villas, a hotel)

Most holiday-villa buyers use a leasehold. It is simple, needs no company or residence permit, and is signed before a notary. Three points matter:

  1. It has an end date. When the term ends the right returns to the landowner unless it has been extended. That is why the extension clause matters so much.
  2. The extension should be written into the contract from day one, with its conditions. A 23-year lease with a 10-year option is not worth the same as a 23-year lease without one.
  3. It can be sold, assigned and inherited if the contract allows it. Check that yours says so explicitly.

What about the "freehold" some portals advertise? A foreigner can only get something similar through a PT PMA or, in some cases, Hak Pakai. Any structure that puts the land in the name of a "trusted" Indonesian (a nominee) is void under Indonesian agrarian law and does not protect you. More in our leasehold vs freehold guide.

Off-plan or finished?

Buying off-plan (before or during construction) has two advantages: a lower entry price and the ability to pay in stages while the property is built. In exchange, you wait and you take on the developer's delivery risk. Buying finished costs more, but you see what you buy and can rent from the first month.

How you pay: no mortgage, staged payments

There is no mortgage financing for these buyers in Bali. Indonesian banks do not lend to foreigners against a leasehold, and banks abroad do not accept a right over Indonesian property as collateral. You buy with your own funds.

What you do get is staged payments: on an off-plan purchase you do not pay everything at signing. You pay a reservation, a signing payment that starts construction, and the rest in instalments on dates fixed in the contract that follow construction progress. The final payment is due at handover. Our contracts are priced in euros, so the price does not move with the rupiah.

Projected returns: from gross to net

Holiday-villa yield is calculated like this:

Gross annual income = average nightly rate × nights booked per year

Gross yield = gross income ÷ purchase price

Subtract running costs to get to net:

  • Rental management: Bali managers typically charge 10% (monthly rentals) to 15% or more (holiday rentals) of revenue.
  • Booking platform commissions.
  • Cleaning, laundry, utilities, internet and pool maintenance.
  • Local taxes on tourist accommodation.
  • Replacing furniture over time.

Our projections range from 15% to 28% gross per year depending on type, area and occupancy scenario. They are projections, not guarantees. Net will be lower than gross, and some years will be better than others. Do not forget depreciation either: with a 23-year lease, the price you pay is "used up" over the term, and you should mentally deduct that from the yield.

Be wary of anyone promising a guaranteed return: several Bali developers who did so have ended up in disputes with their buyers.

Taxes on Bali property

We are not tax advisers and every case is different. This is the general framework to discuss with your adviser.

In Indonesia

  • When buying a leasehold you pay notary and formalities. Land taxes (such as PBB, the annual land and building tax) are normally borne by the landowner; check what your contract says.
  • On rental income there is a local tax on tourist accommodation of up to 10% of revenue in Badung regency, where Uluwatu and Canggu are, plus Indonesian income tax, whose form depends on how the villa is operated (through a licensed manager, a company, etc.).

In your home country

  • If you are tax resident in Australia, the UK, Spain or most EU countries, you are taxed on worldwide income: Bali rental income must be declared at home.
  • Indonesia has double tax treaties with many countries, including Australia, the UK and Spain, so tax paid in Indonesia can usually be credited under the treaty rules.
  • Some countries also require you to report foreign assets above a threshold, and wealth and inheritance rules vary.

Always speak to a tax adviser before buying: the structure you choose (individual, company) changes the outcome.

How to buy from abroad, step by step

You do not need to travel to Bali to buy, though many buyers visit at least once. The typical process:

  1. Choose a project and unit. By video call, with plans, renders, construction photos and the template contract. If you travel, you visit the site and the area.
  2. Reservation. A fixed amount holds the unit while documents are reviewed.
  3. Legal checks. The notary and PPAT (the official who handles land-rights deeds in Indonesia) checks the land certificate, the owner's identity and the chain of rights. See what the PPAT notary does in a Bali purchase.
  4. Contract. You receive the full contract before signing. Ours are bilingual, article by article, so you know exactly what you sign.
  5. Signing before the notary. In person in Bali or remotely with a power of attorney. The power of attorney is signed before a notary in your country, apostilled (Indonesia joined the Hague Apostille Convention in 2022) and translated by a sworn translator.
  6. Staged payments. Transfers in euros according to the contract schedule.
  7. Progress tracking. Construction reports with dated photos in your client area.
  8. Handover. Unit inspection, keys, furniture if you ordered it, and rental launch.

Real risks and how to protect yourself

Investing in Bali carries specific risks. None is a reason not to invest, but all should be checked before signing.

1. The developer does not finish

The main off-plan risk. To reduce it:

  • Check the developer has a registered Indonesian company and finished projects you can visit.
  • Ask for dated construction photos and visit the site if you can.
  • Only pay into the account named in the contract, never into personal accounts of intermediaries without contractual backing.
  • Make sure the contract sets a handover date, a grace period and compensation if it is exceeded.

2. Land or zoning problems

Every plot in Bali has a permitted use under the zoning plan, and not all allow tourist rentals.

  • The notary must verify the land certificate (SHM or SHGB) and its owner.
  • Ask about zoning and permit status: land-use validation (KKPR), building permit (PBG), certificate of fitness for use (SLF) and tourist accommodation licence (Pondok Wisata or equivalent). Some of these take months to process; what matters is that the use is permitted and the file is under way.

3. Rental regulation changes

Bali is tightening control of holiday rentals: booking platforms increasingly require a valid licence and the authorities are pursuing nominee structures. For investors this is good news in the medium term, as it pushes out irregular supply, but it means buying on correctly zoned land and renting through a licensed operator.

4. Lease term

A 20-30-year right loses value as it approaches its end. Make sure the extension is agreed in the contract and the right can be sold and inherited.

5. Currency

If you buy in euros, the price is fixed. Rental income, however, is usually earned in rupiah or dollars, and its value in your currency will vary.

6. Lower occupancy than expected

Yield depends on occupancy. A good location, a distinctive design and professional management protect it most. Do not base your numbers on high-season months.

7. Natural hazards

Bali is a seismic and volcanic area with a rainy season. Read about natural hazards in Bali and ask about the construction warranty (one year from handover on our units).

Bali versus other options

  • Versus Lombok: cheaper land, but much lower tourist demand, fewer flights and a market still taking shape. For a first-time investor, Bali offers a more proven rental track record.
  • Versus Thailand: foreigners can own condominium units outright there, but land for villas faces restrictions similar to Bali's. Bali stands out for its private-pool villa product and its villa-rental market.
  • Versus Dubai: more liquidity and full ownership for foreigners, but a much higher entry ticket and a different guest and product.
  • Versus a holiday let at home: a lower entry ticket and fewer local licence caps, in exchange for a time-limited right and distance.

Frequently asked questions

Can foreigners buy property in Bali?

Yes, through a leasehold: a long-term right to use and rent out the property, usually 20 to 35 years and extendable, signed before a notary. Foreigners cannot own freehold land in their own name, but can buy, rent out, sell and inherit the leasehold if the contract allows it.

How much do I need to invest in Bali?

Developer projects start at around €90,000 for a one-bedroom studio or apartment. Add notary fees (around 1%), bank charges and, if you want it, a furniture package.

Can I get a mortgage to buy in Bali?

No. Indonesian banks do not lend to foreigners against a leasehold and foreign banks do not accept it as collateral. You buy from your own funds; on new builds you pay in stages during construction.

Is buying property in Bali a good investment?

It can be, with the right checks: a registered developer with delivered projects, a land certificate verified by the notary, zoning that allows tourist rentals, a contract with dates and warranties, and payments only to the contract account. Returns are projections, never guaranteed.

What rental yield can a Bali villa make?

It depends on area, type, occupancy and management. Our projections range from 15% to 28% gross per year depending on the scenario; net yield after management, maintenance and taxes is lower. These are projections, not guarantees.

Do I need to travel to Bali to buy?

No. You can sign remotely with a power of attorney made before a notary in your country, apostilled and translated by a sworn translator. Many buyers still visit once to see the site and the area.

What happens when the leasehold ends?

The right returns to the landowner unless it has been extended. That is why the extension option should be written into the contract from the start, with its terms.
